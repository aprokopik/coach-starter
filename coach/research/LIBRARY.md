# Evidence library

Evidence entries that ship with the kit. Method: `PROTOCOL.md`. This file is reference data, not instructions.

**Labels**
- Certainty: how strong the evidence itself is.
- Fit: confidence in applying it to the athlete.
- Access: FT = full text read, ABS = abstract only, SEC = secondary summary only.
- Status:
  - Applied: a current rule rests on it.
  - Informs: context for judgment.
  - Proposed: a rule change waiting for the athlete; see `QUEUE.md`.
  - Watch: mixed or emerging evidence.
  - Superseded: kept for history.
  - New: added by a scan, not yet reported.

## TR: Endurance training and performance

### TR-01 Rosenblat et al. 2025: training intensity distribution, individual-participant network meta-analysis
- Sports Med 55:655–673. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 13 studies, n=348. Polarized and pyramidal did not differ for VO2max (SMD −0.06) or time trial (−0.05). Recreational athletes trended toward pyramidal (SMD 0.32, ns) and competitive athletes toward polarized (−0.31, ns).
- Limits: small arms, 85% male, intensity classified by HR time in zone.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-02 Silva Oliveira et al. 2024: polarized vs other distributions, meta-analysis
- Sports Med 54(8):2071–2095. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 17 studies, n=437. Polarized gave slightly more VO2peak gain (SMD 0.24), only in programs under 12 weeks or in highly trained athletes. No time-trial difference (−0.01).
- Certainty moderate (TT), low (subgroups) · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-03 Milanović et al. 2015: HIT vs continuous training for VO2max
- Sports Med. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0365-0 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 28 trials, n=723 adults aged 18 to 45. VO2max rose +5.5 mL/kg/min with HIT and +4.9 with continuous training; HIT beat continuous by 1.2. The edge was larger with longer reps and longer programs. Also Wen 2019, J Sci Med Sport 22(8):941, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2019.01.013: only long-interval, high-volume HIIT beat continuous training (SMD 0.65 to 1.07).
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-04 Rosenblat et al. 2020: HIIT vs sprint intervals for time trial
- Sports Med 50(6):1145–1161. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01264-1 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 6 studies. Long HIIT (reps of 4 min or more) improved time trial about 2% more than sprint intervals. VO2max did not differ.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-05 Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024: strength training and running economy
- Sports Med 54(4):895–932. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: n=652 trained runners. Running economy improved with high-load lifting (ES −0.27), plyometrics (−0.31, mainly at 12 km/h or slower) and combined methods (−0.43). Light-load and isometric work had no effect.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-06 Llanos-Lagos et al. 2025: heavy strength training in cyclists
- Eur J Appl Physiol 126(1):193–222. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-025-05883-2 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 17 studies, n=262. Heavy lifting at 80% 1RM or more, about 2 sessions/week: cycling performance g=0.46, efficiency g=0.35, VO2max unchanged. Gains needed about 8 weeks or more of 3 to 4 sets across about 4 leg exercises. Supported by Beattie 2014, Sports Med 44(6):845, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0157-y.
- Limits: GRADE low; strength mostly replaced endurance time; strength-naive subjects.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-07 Bosquet et al. 2007: tapering meta-analysis
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 39(8):1358–1365. https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 27 studies. Best effect from a 2-week taper (ES 0.59) with volume cut 41 to 60%, an exponential reduction, and intensity and frequency kept.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-08 Wang et al. 2023: tapering in endurance athletes
- PLoS One 18(5):e0282838. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282838 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 14 studies. Time trial improved (SMD −0.45). A pre-taper overload plus taper beat a taper alone.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-09 Düking et al. 2021: HRV-guided training with wearables
- J Sci Med Sport 24(11):1180–1192. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2021.04.012 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 8 studies, n=198. Submaximal markers improved more with HRV guidance (g=0.30). Performance (g=0.08) and VO2peak (g=0.17) were not different.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-10 Manresa-Rocamora et al. 2021: HRV-guided training, methodological review
- Int J Environ Res Public Health 18:10299. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph181910299 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: n=199. VO2max (0.13) and performance (0.20) were not different; vagal HRV improved more (0.50), with fewer non-responders. Granero-Gallegos 2020 (https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17217999) found larger effects but with I² of 94%; on the watch list.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-11 Bini et al. 2022: running after cycling
- J Sci Med Sport 25(10):861–866. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2022.07.006 · SR · ABS
- Finding: 39 mostly lab studies. Running after cycling is usually impaired. Changing cadence or intensity gave conflicting results.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-12 Knechtle et al. 2015: what predicts Ironman performance
- Open Access J Sports Med 6:277–290. https://doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S85310 · narrative review · FT
- Finding: training volume predicted race time better than training speed. Bike and run splits drive overall time; the swim does not. Previous race experience predicts 70.3 time.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-13 Wu et al. 2014: pacing in triathlon
- Open Access J Sports Med 5:223–234. https://doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S44392 · narrative review · FT
- Finding: the swim is about 10% of 70.3 time. Even bike pacing works best. Glycogen depletion limits long-course performance.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-14 Racinais et al. 2023: IOC consensus on sport in the heat
- Br J Sports Med 57:8–25. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-105942 · consensus · FT
- Finding: acclimate with 60 to 90 min/day for at least 2 weeks (4 or more sessions/week to induce, 2/week to maintain). Avoid starting more than 2% dehydrated. For events over 1 h: sodium 0.5 to 0.7 g/L and carbohydrate 30 to 60 g/h. Daanen 2018 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0808-x): adaptations decay about 2.5% per day, and re-induction is fast.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-15 Iannetta et al. 2020: fixed-percentage intensity prescription vs thresholds
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 52(2):466–473. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002147 · cross-sectional (n=100: 46 women, 54 men) · ABS
- Finding: the lactate threshold fell anywhere from 60 to 90% of HRmax, and maximal lactate steady state from 75 to 97% of HRmax. Fixed %HRmax or %VO2max anchors "conform poorly" to individual intensity domains.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied

### TR-16 Spiering et al. 2021: minimal dose to maintain endurance and strength
- J Strength Cond Res 35(5):1449–1458. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/ · narrative review (draws on Hickson and Mujika detraining studies) · ABS
- Finding: endurance performance maintained for up to 15 weeks with training frequency as low as 2 sessions/week, or volume cut by 33 to 66% (about 13 to 26 min per session), provided exercise intensity is maintained. Mostly general populations.
- Certainty moderate (narrative review of small studies) · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-17 Currier et al. 2026: ACSM position stand on resistance training prescription (overview of reviews)
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4):851–872. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897 · position stand (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants) · SEC (author-shared PDF summary and ACSM infographic; full tables not read)
- Finding: train all major muscle groups at least 2 days/week. Strength: 2 to 3 sets per exercise, heavier loads (about 80% 1RM or more) give more strength (dose-response). Hypertrophy: about 10 or more sets per muscle group per week (dose-response); light (30% 1RM) and heavy loads build similar muscle when effort is matched. Training to failure is not necessary. Going from nothing to any lifting is the biggest gain. Replaces the 2009 ACSM progression model.
- Limits: most underlying data come from untrained or novice lifters; concurrent (strength plus endurance) effects could not be judged for lack of data.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-18 Held et al. 2026: maximizing adaptations in concurrent training (umbrella review)
- Sports Med. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02401-y · umbrella review of 17 meta-analyses (144 studies, 1,492 participants, mean AMSTAR 10.4) · FULL (author PDF)
- Finding: concurrent vs endurance only: aerobic capacity equal (SMD 0.05), strength higher (0.59). Concurrent vs lifting only: aerobic capacity higher (0.77), strength (0.17), power (0.21) and hypertrophy (−0.04) trivially different. Lifting before endurance favoured strength (SMD 1.69, very wide error) with aerobic gains unaffected. Heavier loads gave more strength under concurrent training.
- Limits: mostly untrained or recreational adults, about 10 week studies; few trained athletes, where interference may be larger and lands on strength more than endurance.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-19 Pelland et al. 2025: resistance training dose response, weekly volume and frequency
- Sports Med 56(2):481–505. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w · Bayesian meta-regressions, 67 studies, 2,058 participants (mean age 25) · SEC (blog summary and abstract; full text blocked)
- Finding: more weekly sets give more strength and muscle with diminishing returns. Strength flattens after about 5 to 6 direct sets a week of a movement; hypertrophy needs at least about 4 to 6 and keeps rising toward 10 to 20 sets per muscle a week. Frequency adds strength (1 to 2 to 3 sessions, smaller each step) but not muscle once volume is equal.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-20 Lixandrão et al. 2024: higher volume offsets hypertrophy non-response in older adults
- J Appl Physiol. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00670.2023 · within-subject RCT, 85 older adults (about 68 y), 10 weeks knee extension 2x/week, 1 set one leg vs 4 sets the other · ABS (summaries)
- Finding: 51 of 85 did not grow on 1 set; nearly all grew on 4 sets. Responders also gained more size on 4 sets; strength gains similar.
- Limits: older, untrained, one exercise.
- Certainty moderate · Fit low · New

### TR-21 Mattioni Maturana et al. 2026: dose response of exercise duration on VO2max, HIIT vs MICT
- Sports Med. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02528-y · dose-response meta-analysis, 69 RCTs, 2,387 participants · FULL (author PDF)
- Finding: HIIT beat MICT by 1.2 mL/kg/min (d 0.38). HIIT reaches 80% of its VO2max effect at about 33 min a week of session time (about 15 min of hard work); MICT needs about 133 min and had not plateaued at 200 min.
- Limits: mostly sedentary or low fitness; intensity anchored to %HRmax or %VO2max, not thresholds.
- Certainty moderate · Fit low to moderate · Applied

### TR-22 Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024: strength training methods for middle and long distance runners
- Sports Med (2024). https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02018-z · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 38 studies, 894 runners aged 17 to 40. Plyometric jump training alone did not clearly improve running performance (ES −0.21, p=0.06) or running economy (ES −0.12, p=0.17). Runner traits and programme length did not change the effects. (TR-18 to TR-21 are held for the concurrent training papers another thread is adding.)
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-23 Refalo et al. 2023: proximity to failure and muscle growth
- Sports Med (2023). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/ · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 15 studies. Sets to failure gave only a trivial edge in hypertrophy over non-failure sets (ES 0.19, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.37); momentary failure itself gave none (ES 0.12, ns). Higher vs moderate velocity loss did not differ. Closer to failure is not always more growth.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-24 Lopez et al. 2021: heavy vs moderate vs light loads for muscle and strength
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 53(6):1206–1216. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585 · SR with network MA · FT (university repository copy, jyx.jyu.fi)
- Finding: 28 studies, n=747 healthy adults (mean age 23), all sets to failure; loads grouped as low (<60% 1RM), moderate (60–79%), high (≥80%). Hypertrophy did not differ: high vs low SMD 0.12 (95% CI −0.06 to 0.29), moderate vs low 0.20 (−0.04 to 0.44), high vs moderate −0.09 (−0.33 to 0.16). 1RM strength: high vs low 0.60 (0.38 to 0.82), moderate vs low 0.34 (0.05 to 0.62), high vs moderate 0.26 (−0.02 to 0.54, ns); 98.2% probability that high load gives the largest strength gain.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-25 Singer et al. 2024: rest interval between sets and muscle growth
- Front Sports Act Living 6:1429789. https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789 · SR with Bayesian MA · FT
- Finding: 9 studies, 19 measurements (about 185 participants, summed from the study table). Short rest (≤60 s) vs longer: pairwise SMD for thigh 0.17 (95% CrI −0.13 to 0.43), arm 0.13 (−0.27 to 0.51), whole body −0.08 (−0.45 to 0.29), i.e. a small edge for resting more than 60 s. Authors found no appreciable added benefit from resting more than about 90 s; evidence beyond that is uncertain.
- Certainty low (few small studies) · Fit high · New

### TR-26 Moesgaard et al. 2022: periodized vs non-periodized lifting at equal volume
- Sports Med 52:1647–1666 (July 2022 issue; issue number not shown on the page read). https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 35 studies, volume-equated. Periodized vs non-periodized: 1RM ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.57), hypertrophy ES 0.13 (−0.10 to 0.36, ns). Undulating vs linear: 1RM ES 0.31 (0.02 to 0.61) favouring undulating, driven by trained lifters (0.61, 0.00 to 1.22; untrained 0.06, −0.20 to 0.31); hypertrophy no difference (0.05, −0.20 to 0.29).
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-27 Eihara et al. 2022: heavy lifting vs plyometrics for running economy and time trials
- Sports Med Open 8:138. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-022-00511-1 · SR/MA · FT (published article plus author thesis)
- Finding: 22 studies (heavy resistance about 216 runners, plyometrics about 263; the paper's own study split is inconsistent). Running economy: heavy resistance g −0.32 (95% CI −0.55 to −0.10, small); plyometrics g −0.13 (−0.47 to 0.21, trivial). Time trial: heavy g −0.24 (the printed CI is internally inconsistent, Results say it crosses zero); plyometrics g −0.17 (−0.27 to −0.06). Author's thesis subgroups (not in the abstract): heavy work over 10–14 weeks g −0.45 (−0.83 to −0.08) vs 6–8 weeks −0.21 (ns); heavy at ≥90% 1RM or ≤4RM g −0.31 (−0.61 to −0.02).
- What it adds to TR-05/TR-22: a head-to-head that ranks heavy work above jumps for economy and suggests the effect needs 10 or more weeks to show.
- Certainty low (inconsistent reporting, small trials) · Fit moderate · New

### TR-28 Schumann et al. 2022: concurrent training interference, same session vs separated
- Sports Med (online 2021, print 2022). https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7 · SR/MA · FT (author repository, researchonline.jcu.edu.au; volume and pages not shown on the copy read, SEC for those)
- Finding: 43 studies, n=1,090. Concurrent vs strength-only: maximal strength SMD −0.06 (95% CI −0.20 to 0.09, ns), hypertrophy −0.01 (−0.16 to 0.18, ns), explosive strength −0.28 (−0.48 to −0.08). Explosive-strength interference appeared when both were done in the same session (≤20 min apart, SMD −0.31, −0.62 to −0.01) but not when sessions were at least 3 h apart. Order (endurance first or strength first) did not change the result. Cycling showed interference on explosive strength (−0.44, −0.86 to −0.01) that disappeared after removing one influential study; running did not.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · New

### TR-29 Lazarczuk et al. 2022: how healthy lower-limb tendons adapt to loading
- Sports Med 52:2405–2429. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01695-y · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 61 articles, 763 participants (33 Achilles studies). Pooled training effect on stiffness SMD 0.74 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.86), Young's modulus 0.82 (0.58 to 1.07), cross-sectional area 0.22 (0.12 to 0.33). Resistance training was the only type with clear changes in all three (stiffness 0.80, 0.67 to 0.94). Isometric, eccentric and concentric-eccentric work all raised stiffness with no difference between modes; only eccentric work raised CSA (SMD 0.58). Jump training gave a smaller stiffness gain (0.49, 0.22 to 0.77). Concurrent (resistance plus aerobic) training showed no stiffness change (2 groups only) and smaller modulus gains. High tendon strain (~5%, Achilles, isometric) beat low strain (~3%), from three groups only. Adults ≥60 y adapted less (stiffness 0.40, 0.02 to 0.78).
- Certainty moderate (small trials, method variation) · Fit high · New

### TR-30 Balachandran et al. 2022: power training vs traditional strength training in older adults
- JAMA Netw Open 5(5):e2211623. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.11623 · SR/MA · ABS (institutional repository record)
- Finding: 20 RCTs, n=566 community-living older adults. Power training (fast concentric) vs traditional slow strength training: physical function SMD 0.30 (95% CI 0.05 to 0.54; 13 studies, n=383); self-reported function 0.38 (−0.62 to 1.37, ns). GRADE low certainty.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · New

### TR-31 Coleman et al. 2024: does a one-week deload help or hurt?
- PeerJ 12:e16777. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.16777 · RCT (no review exists on deloads) · FT
- Finding: 39 resistance-trained young adults, 9 weeks; one group stopped all lifting in week 5. Muscle thickness did not differ (e.g. vastus lateralis 0.08 mm, 95% CrI −1.5 to 1.6). Squat 1RM gain slightly favoured continuous training (−4.5 kg, CrI −10.4 to 2.8; deload 92.8 to 105.8 kg vs continuous 95.9 to 112.3 kg); isometric knee extension also leaned towards continuous training; jump unchanged.
- Certainty low (single small RCT) · Fit moderate · New

### TR-32 Grgic 2022: how fast muscle size is lost after stopping lifting (older adults)
- Int J Environ Res Public Health 19(21):14048. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph192114048 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 6 studies, 8 groups (about 80 participants). Muscle size after detraining vs end of training: overall d −0.83 (95% CI −1.30 to −0.36). 12–24 weeks off: d −0.60 (−1.21 to 0.01, ns); 31–52 weeks off: d −1.11 (−1.75 to −0.47). Muscle loss is slow over the first months and becomes clear only after more than about half a year.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · New

### TR-33 Encarnação et al. 2022: detraining and strength retention
- Muscles 1(1):1–15. https://doi.org/10.3390/muscles1010001 · SR with MA of 1RM · FT
- Finding: 20 studies. Strength fell after stopping (chest press and leg press g about −1.1 to −1.7 after 16–24 weeks off) but stayed above pre-training baseline at 16–24 weeks; by 32–48 weeks it was no longer different from baseline. Total n not reported in the text read; small new journal.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · New

### TR-34 Haugen et al. 2023: free weights vs machines
- BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 15(1) (2023; article number not shown on the DOAJ record read, SEC for it). https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-023-00713-4 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 13 studies, n=1,016. No difference for hypertrophy (SMD −0.055, 95% CI −0.397 to 0.287), dynamic strength (0.084, −0.106 to 0.273), isometric strength (−0.079, −0.432 to 0.273) or countermovement jump (−0.209, −0.597 to 0.179). Strength was test-specific: free-weight tests favoured free-weight training (SMD −0.210, −0.391 to −0.029).
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · New

### TR-35 Liao et al. 2022: unilateral vs bilateral training
- Biol Sport 39(3):485–497. https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2022.107024 · SR/MA · ABS (lida.sport-iat.de; author list not shown there, SEC for authors)
- Finding: 14 studies, n=392 aged 16 to 26. Unilateral training improved unilateral jump more (ES 0.89, 95% CI 0.52 to 1.26); bilateral training improved bilateral strength more (−0.43, −0.71 to −0.14). No difference for unilateral strength (0.26, −0.03 to 0.55), bilateral jump, sprint (−0.12, −0.46 to 0.21) or change of direction (0.31, −0.01 to 0.63).
- Certainty low to moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-36 Roth et al. 2022: lifting volume and lean-mass sparing during a diet in trained lifters
- Eur J Appl Physiol 122(5):1129–1151. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-022-04896-5 · SR (no pooled MA) · ABS (lida.sport-iat.de; authors from the repository title listing)
- Finding: 15 studies in resistance-trained athletes in caloric restriction; only 4 reported enough data to compute weekly sets. Studies using about 10 or more sets per muscle per week, or increasing volume during the diet, showed low to no lean-mass loss, while studies that cut volume lost more. Authors judge evidence insufficient to call higher volume better overall; deficit size, training history and pre-diet volume modify the effect.
- What it adds to NU-28: in a deficit, holding or raising lifting volume (not cutting it) is the lever that protects muscle.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### TR-37 Yang et al. 2025: which interval format raises VO2max most in athletes (network meta-analysis)
- BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 17:156. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-025-01191-6 · systematic review with pairwise and network meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42023435021) · FT
- Finding: 51 studies, 1,261 athletes (mixed sports, McKay tiers 2 to 4). Against usual training, VO2max rose with repeated-sprint training g 1.04 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.32), HIIT g 1.01 (0.76 to 1.25) and sprint intervals (SIT) g 0.69 (0.39 to 1.00); the three did not differ from each other (p > 0.05). Continuous training ranked last (g 0.29). HIIT showed an inverted-U dose response, best at about 140 s work with a work:rest ratio of about 0.85 (for example 140 s on, 165 s easy). SIT gains disappeared when recovery exceeded about 97 s.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-38 Schoenmakers et al. 2026: time spent near VO2max in different interval sessions
- BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 18:358. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01766-x · systematic review and meta-analysis (86 articles, 239 HIIT protocols) · FT
- Finding: work-interval length changed time at or near VO2max (short vs medium vs long, SMD 0.45, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.75; I2 76%). Post hoc, long intervals beat short (g 0.87 for time above 90% VO2max; 1.15 above 95%) and medium ones (g 1.06; 1.52). Varying intensity within each rep (fast start then hold) beat even pacing: SMD 0.80 (0.40 to 1.18), about 165 s (97 to 223 s) more time near VO2max per session. Active vs passive recovery made no difference (SMD -0.15, -0.42 to 0.13).
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · New

### TR-39 Norte, Steele and Wright 2023: interval training in trained cyclists
- Int J Strength Cond 3(1). https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v3i1.271 · exploratory systematic review, multilevel and network meta-analysis (not pre-registered) · FT (pages SEC; online-only journal, no page range shown)
- Finding: 14 studies, 302 trained cyclists (VO2max 47 to 73 mL/kg/min). Added interval training vs endurance-only control: absolute VO2max g 0.28 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.40), absolute peak aerobic power g 0.38 (0.15 to 0.61), both low-certainty GRADE; threshold power g 0.46 (-0.24 to 1.17) and time trial g 0.96 (-0.81 to 2.73) were imprecise. Single-study gains in VO2max were about 3 to 10% for SIT, short and long HIIT alike; the network found no clearly superior format (short HIIT was the only one separating from control). Programme length did not moderate gains (beta 0.04 per week, -0.07 to 0.15).
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · New

### TR-40 Casado et al. 2023 (with Talsnes et al. 2024): lactate-guided "double threshold" (Norwegian model)
- Int J Environ Res Public Health 20(5):3782. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20053782 · narrative review · ABS. Companion trial: Talsnes et al. 2024, Front Physiol 15:1428536, https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2024.1428536 · randomised crossover · FT
- Finding: Casado: the model is 3 to 4 lactate-guided threshold interval sessions plus one VO2max session a week on top of high easy volume (150 to 180 km/week in the elite runners described), with rep pace set to blood lactate about 2 to 4.5 mmol/L; the rationale is high speed with fast recovery. A companion systematic review (Sci J Sport Perform 2023;3(1):38-46, https://doi.org/10.55860/NBXV4075, authors SEC; 7 articles, 13 elite runners) found 75 to 80% of training at low intensity and 2 to 4 threshold sessions a week. No trial in non-elite athletes exists. Talsnes (14 trained athletes, VO2max 69 mL/kg/min): splitting 6 x 10 min at 90% of 4 mmol speed into two 3 x 10 min sessions 6.5 h apart lowered internal load about 20% (session load 743 vs 929 AU), and HR drift within the single session (+2.8%) vanished in the split format, with less next-morning fatigue and soreness.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · New

### TR-41 Storoschuk et al. 2025: "zone 2" is not uniquely best for mitochondria or fitness
- Sports Med 55(7):1611-1624. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02261-y · narrative review · ABS (key detail from a secondary summary, marked below)
- Finding: the claim that zone 2 (below the first lactate threshold, about 2 mmol/L) is the optimal intensity for mitochondrial and fat-oxidation capacity rests mainly on observational data from high-volume elite athletes. In the general population, higher intensities raise mitochondrial content and VO2max at least as well per hour; current evidence does not identify zone 2 as optimal, and training above zone 2 matters most when weekly volume is limited. Secondary summary (SEC, not checked in the paper): intensities below about 60% of maximal aerobic power do not reliably raise mitochondrial content in untrained people.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### TR-42 Sánchez-Jiménez et al. 2025 (with Hunter et al. 2025): durability, how power fades after prior work
- Eur J Appl Physiol (2025, volume and pages SEC). https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-025-05885-0 · systematic review, no meta-analysis (OSF registered) · FT. Methods companion: Hunter et al. 2025, Exp Physiol 110(11):1612-1624, https://doi.org/10.1113/EP092120 · review · ABS
- Finding: 21 studies, 585 cyclists. Power output fell 10 to 20% after 2.5 to 15 kJ/kg of prior work above critical power, while work below critical power typically cost under 5% even at high volumes (for example -1.6 to -3.0% after 15 kJ/kg and -6.0 to -9.7% after 45 kJ/kg of mixed racing work in pros). Less-trained riders lost power sooner (U23 after 2.5 to 5 kJ/kg above CP vs pros after 5 to 7.5). Intensity of prior work, not total kJ, is the main driver. Hunter: durability = resilience of thresholds and performance during prolonged exercise; it should be tested after a standardised fatiguing bout, since longer and harder pre-loads cause bigger drops.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### TR-43 Kobayashi et al. 2026: carbon-plated shoes and running economy
- Front Sports Act Living 7:1710224. https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1710224 · systematic review and meta-analysis of crossover trials (PROSPERO CRD42024520736) · FT
- Finding: 14 studies (13 pooled), 271 runners. Plated vs non-plated shoes: oxygen cost of running -2.88% (95% CI -4.57 to -1.19%; k = 4) and metabolic cost -2.64% (-4.09 to -1.11%; k = 9), moderate certainty. The plate cannot be separated from the foam and geometry. No injury outcomes were pooled.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-44 Anderson et al. 2022: changing running step rate (cadence)
- Sports Med Open 8:112. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-022-00504-0 · systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42020167657) · FT
- Finding: 37 studies, mostly immediate-effect lab studies in young recreational runners. Raising step rate shortens the step (SMD 0.93, 95% CI 0.49 to 1.37, moderate) and changes knee mechanics: peak knee flexion (0.66, 0.40 to 0.92, strong evidence), knee extensor moment (0.50, 0.18 to 0.81, moderate), hip adduction (0.40, 0.11 to 0.69, moderate), patellofemoral joint stress (0.56, 0.07 to 1.05, limited), all in the direction of less knee and hip load. Vertical loading rates did not change (average 0.24, -0.23 to 0.70). Lowering step rate did the opposite (longer contact, bigger braking impulse). Effects on running economy rest on very limited evidence. Injury and performance: "insufficient evidence"; two small uncontrolled studies showed less patellofemoral pain at 4 and 12 weeks.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-45 Feng et al. 2024: "live low, train high" hypoxic training for sea-level performance (network meta-analysis)
- PLoS One 19:e0297007. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0297007 · systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis · ABS (participant total SEC; not given in the abstract)
- Finding: 56 studies in athletes (search to June 2023). For aerobic performance (53 studies) only two hypoxic formats beat the same training in normal air: long intervals of more than 5 min in hypoxia, SMD 0.78 (95% CrI 0.52 to 1.05), and repeated sprints of 5 to 10 s in hypoxia, SMD 0.30 (0.10 to 0.50). Passive intermittent hypoxic exposure (sleeping tents, breathing devices at rest) and continuous easy training in hypoxia were unlikely to help.
- Certainty low · Fit low · New

### TR-46 Fernandes et al. 2025: older adults do not get more muscle damage from a hard bout
- J Aging Phys Act 33(6):606-624. https://doi.org/10.1123/japa.2024-0165 · systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression · ABS (CIs and total n not in the abstract: SEC)
- Finding: 36 studies comparing younger and older groups after the same damaging exercise. Loss of muscle function did not differ by age at 24 h, 72 h or peak (SMD -0.16 to -0.35, n.s.); soreness was greater in the younger groups (SMD -0.34 to -0.62) and so was creatine kinase at 24 h and peak (SMD -0.32). Meta-regression found no age effect on other outcomes. The authors conclude advancing age is not linked to greater exercise-induced muscle damage.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### TR-47 de Waal et al. 2021: what physiology predicts trail running performance
- Int J Sports Physiol Perform 16(3):325-332. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2020-0812 · systematic review (7 studies) · ABS
- Finding: performance correlated most often with VO2max (71% of studies; mean r = -0.50), lactate threshold (57%; r = -0.48), speed at VO2max (43%; r = -0.68), running economy (43%; r = -0.31), body fat (43%; r = 0.55) and age (43%; r = 0.52). The classic road model (VO2max, threshold, economy) explains trail results less well than road results; race profile and terrain skills matter.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### TR-48 Bontemps et al. 2025 (with Lu et al. 2025): downhill running, muscle damage and the repeated bout effect
- Eur J Sport Sci 25(3):e12240. https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.12240 · training study, repeated measures (n = 12, untrained) · ABS. Context: Lu et al. 2025, Front Bioeng Biotechnol 13:1690023, https://doi.org/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1690023 · narrative review · FT
- Finding: Bontemps: 12 untrained adults (VO2max 49 mL/kg/min) did two blocks of five downhill treadmill runs (15 to 30 min at 60 to 65% VO2max). A standardised 15 min downhill test cut quadriceps strength similarly after 0, 5 and 10 prior sessions (about -14%, -11%, -9%; no difference between time points), and central and peripheral fatigue were unchanged, but quadriceps soreness fell from 29.6 to 15.4 mm after 5 sessions (n.s.) and to 8.7 mm after 10 (p = 0.01). Lu (citing Bontemps 2020): after a downhill run knee-extensor strength falls 14 to 55% and plantar-flexor strength 15 to 25%, with strength loss lasting several days; prior downhill exposure is one of the most effective protections; on descents, cadence within about 5% of preferred minimised energy cost and impact.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### TR-49 Nikolaidis et al. 2023: which split predicts 70.3 finish time in age-groupers

- Finding: the run split correlated most with overall time (r = 0.89 women, 0.90 men), then the bike (r = 0.87 women, 0.86 men); the swim much less. ### TR-50 ACSM 2009 position stand: progression models in resistance training (with the NSCA 2 for 2 rule)
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687-708. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/ · position stand · abstract and secondary summaries. NSCA Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning, program design chapter, via study summaries.
- Finding: when training at a set load, raise it 2 to 10% once the person can do 1 to 2 reps more than the target. The NSCA's more cautious 2 for 2 rule adds load after 2 extra reps on the last set in two workouts in a row: 2 to 5 lb upper body and 5 to 10 lb lower body for less trained lifters, or 2.5 to 10%.
- Certainty moderate (expert consensus) · Fit high · New

### TR-51 Plotkin et al. 2022: load progression vs repetition progression

### TR-52 Graham and Cleather 2021: autoregulation by reps in reserve vs fixed loading
- J Strength Cond Res 35(9):2451-2456. https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/3067/3/Graham-Cleather-Autoregulation-Repetitions-in-Reserve.pdf · RCT, 31 trained men, 12 weeks, twice a week · FT (accepted manuscript)

### TR-53 Robinson et al. 2024: proximity to failure, strength and hypertrophy

### IR-02 Impellizzeri et al. 2020 and 2021: ACWR critique

### IR-03 Frandsen et al. 2025: single-session spikes and running injury (Garmin cohort)

### IR-42 George, Sheerin and Reid 2024: return to running after a tibial bone stress injury
- Sports Med 54(9):2247-2265. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02051-y · scoping review (50 sources, mostly reviews and commentaries; level IV evidence) · ABS
- Finding: Five things to clear before running again: bony tenderness gone, pain-free walking, imaging evidence of healing for high-risk sites, strength, functional and loading tests passed, and the contributing factors identified. ### IR-43 Schwellnus et al. 2022: IOC consensus on acute respiratory infections in athletes (part 1)
- Br J Sports Med 56:1066-1088. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-105759 · consensus statement · FT
- Finding: Pooled incidence 4.9 respiratory infections per 1,000 athlete-days (about 1.8 per athlete per year); about 20% cost more than a day of training; mean symptom duration 7 days. ### IR-44 Bellenger, Siegel, Stanley and McGibbon 2023: AIS heart rate variability best practice guide
- Australian Institute of Sport, Nov 2023. https://www.ais.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0013/1130260/HRV-BPG_FINAL.pdf · best practice guide (expert, evidence-informed) · SEC
- Finding: Read HRV as the natural log of RMSSD, as a 7 day rolling average, against the athlete's own rolling 60 day normal (mean plus or minus the smallest worthwhile change, half the coefficient of variation). ### IR-45 Silbernagel et al. 2007: continued activity with a pain monitoring model in Achilles tendinopathy (RCT)

### IR-46 Morton, Fitz-Clarke and Banister 1990: modeling human performance in running (Banister TRIMP)

### NU-01 Thomas et al. 2016: ACSM/AND/DC joint position on nutrition and athletic performance
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 48(3):543–568. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852 · consensus · FT
- Finding:
  - Carbohydrate: 3 to 5 g/kg on light days, 5 to 7 on about 1 h/day, 6 to 10 on 1 to 3 h/day.
  - Protein: 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg, about 0.3 g/kg every 3 to 5 h.
  - During exercise: 30 to 60 g/h carbohydrate for 1 to 2.5 h, up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h.
  - Fluid: keep losses under 2% of body mass.
  - Weight loss: about 250 to 500 kcal/day deficit, in base season.

- J Sci Med Sport 24(2):177-182. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2020.07.016 · systematic review, 9 studies · ABS
- Finding:
  - Jespersen: an even spread of protein across meals was associated with more muscle mass in 3 of 7 studies, with strength in 2 of 7 and with protein synthesis in 1 of 6. The authors judge the evidence sufficient for muscle mass but not for strength or protein turnover. The abstract reports counts only, no effect sizes.
  - Reis: 20 to 40 g of casein before sleep raises overnight muscle protein synthesis. Over prolonged resistance training it may add muscle size and strength in young men. Evidence in older men is limited, and total daily protein was often unequal between groups, which weakens the conclusion.
  - Avoid a dinner-heavy split. Divide the 150 g roughly evenly across the noon, mid-afternoon and last meals.
  - Make the last meal of the window casein-rich (skyr, quark, milk; 30 to 40 g). It is the nearest thing to a pre-sleep feed that 16:8 allows.
  - The total matters more than the timing.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### NU-36 Lim et al. 2021: animal vs plant protein for lean mass and strength
- Nutrients 13(2):661. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13020661 · systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs (18 reviewed, 16 pooled) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Overall, protein source did not change absolute lean mass or strength. Animal protein favoured percent lean mass.
  - Resistance training did not change the results.
  - In adults under 50, animal protein added lean mass:
    - absolute: WMD 0.41 kg, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.74
    - percent: WMD 0.50%, 95% CI 0.00 to 1.01
  - Supporting RCT, Santini et al. 2025 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 22:2568047, n = 44, 12 weeks of training): 45 g/day of a soy and pea blend matched whey for lean mass, vastus lateralis area and leg-press 1RM. All between-group p > 0.05.
  - At about 150 g/day, the source makes little difference.
  - Meals that are mostly plant protein should use blends (soy, or pea plus rice) and carry about 10% more protein.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### NU-37 Gejl and Nybo 2021: carbohydrate periodization ("train low") and performance
- J Int Soc Sports Nutr 18:37. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-021-00435-3 · systematic review and meta-analysis (9 studies, 11 to 26 participants each, 1 to 4 week interventions) · FT
- Finding:
  - Periodized carbohydrate restriction (sleep-low, twice-a-day, fasted training, mixed protocols) did not improve performance compared with high-carbohydrate controls: SMD 0.17, 95% CI -0.15 to 0.49, p = 0.29.
  - Only 2 sleep-low studies (Marquet) reported a benefit, and neither reported a group-by-time interaction.
  - The authors rate the evidence for a performance benefit as weak.
  - Low-intensity rides or runs before the noon first meal are fine, but expect no extra fitness from training fasted.
  - Fuel the quality sessions and the long sessions. Do not add deliberate sleep-low blocks.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · New

### NU-38 Cao et al. 2021 (ketogenic diet) and Brooks et al. 2022 (ketone supplements): no endurance benefit
- Nutrients 13(8):2896. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13082896 · systematic review and meta-analysis (10 studies) · ABS (pooled effect sizes not read)
- Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 32(3):214-225. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2021-0280 · systematic review and meta-analysis (8 studies, n = 80, 77 men) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Ketogenic LCHF diet (Cao): no significant effect on VO2max, time to exhaustion, maximal heart rate or RPE. It reliably lowered RER, meaning more fat oxidation.
  - Acute ketone monoesters and precursors (Brooks):
    - overall Hedges g 0.136, 95% CI -0.195 to 0.467, p = 0.419
    - time to exhaustion: g -0.002, 95% CI -0.312 to 0.308
    - time trial: g 0.057, 95% CI -0.282 to 0.395
    - blood ketones rose in every study
  - There is no case for keto adaptation or ketone esters.
  - Burning more fat does not translate into going faster.

### NU-39 Plews et al. 2026: are ultra-high carbohydrate intakes (over 90 g/h) justified?
- Sports Med 56(10):2367-2388. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02462-z · current opinion / narrative review · ABS
- Finding:
  - The paper defines ultra-high intake as more than 90 g/h.
  - Common explanations (glycogen sparing, blood glucose maintenance, maximal exogenous oxidation) do not fully account for the benefits athletes report.
  - Athletes eat well beyond established oxidation ceilings, and controlled performance data at these rates are limited.
  - Proposed but untested mechanisms: shifts in substrate selection, lactate shuttling and central effects.
  - Carbohydrate oxidation capacity varies widely between individuals.
  - The abstract gives no new numeric recommendation.
  - For a recreational ultra runner at moderate pace, 60 to 90 g/h (trained up gradually, glucose plus fructose) is still the ceiling worth targeting.
  - Going above 90 g/h is unproven and raises gut risk on trails.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### NU-40 Hänisch et al. 2025: low energy availability and performance in male athletes
- J Sci Med Sport 28(2):110-117. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2024.10.008 · scoping review (6 studies, n = 103 men) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Of the 6 studies, 4 were interventions (1 a case study) and 2 were cross-sectional.
  - LEA improved, worsened or did not change strength, endurance and speed, depending on how long and how severe the deficit was and on the athletes' starting status.
  - Methods were weak, and no dose-response could be established.
  - The paper gives no male-specific energy availability threshold.
  - Male data are too thin to set a safe energy availability number.
  - Keep the winter deficit small and on easy days only, as now.
  - Use the IOC REDs signs (IR-09) as a check: falling libido, mood, sleep or performance, or repeated illness.
- Certainty low · Fit high · New

### NU-41 Wan et al. 2024 and Kazeminasab et al. 2025: time-restricted eating and fasting with exercise
- BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med 10(3) (article number not read). https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2023-001831 · systematic review and meta-analysis (15 RCTs, n = 361, exercising adults) · ABS
- Nutrients 17(12):1992. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17121992 · systematic review and meta-analysis (35 RCTs, n = 1266, IF or CR plus exercise vs exercise alone) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Wan 2024 (time-restricted feeding vs normal diet, exercising adults):
    - body mass -1.76 kg, 95% CI -3.40 to -0.13
    - fat mass -1.24 kg, 95% CI -1.87 to -0.61, I² 0%

    - risk of bias: 13 of 15 trials had some concerns
  - Kazeminasab 2025 (IF or CR plus exercise vs exercise alone):
    - VO2max unchanged: SMD 0.005, p = 0.94

    - fat mass -2.858 kg, but fat-free mass also fell by 1.653 kg (p = 0.001)
    - confidence intervals not given in the abstract

  - Losing fat-free mass is the risk when fasting creates a real deficit. Guard against it with 150 g protein, the 2 lifts and a small deficit.

### NU-42 Veniamakis et al. 2022 (sodium in endurance sport) and Miller et al. 2022 (exercise-associated muscle cramps)
- Int J Environ Res Public Health 19(6):3651. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19063651 · systematic review, narrative synthesis · ABS
- J Athl Train 57(1):5-15. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34185846/ · evidence-based review · ABS. The lead author name comes from indexing. The DOI was not read.
- Finding:
  - Veniamakis: both high and low sodium intake are linked to health and performance problems. Sodium imbalance is discussed as a possible contributor to cramp and hyponatraemia. Sodium should be managed together with fluid.
  - Miller: cramp most likely comes from several intrinsic and extrinsic factors combined, not one single cause.
    - Treat an acute cramp with gentle static stretching until it eases.
    - Prevent cramp by addressing each athlete's own risk factors. This is expected to beat generic advice such as "drink more."
  - Neither abstract gives numbers.
  - In races over 4 h, drink to thirst and take sodium with the fluid. About 300 to 600 mg/h is common practice, not a figure from these papers.
  - If the athlete cramps, look first at pacing, fatigue and heat, and only then at salt.

### NU-43 Gardiner et al. 2023: caffeine and later sleep
- Sleep Med Rev 69:101764. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764 · systematic review and meta-analysis (24 studies) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Total sleep time fell by 45 min and sleep efficiency by 7%.
  - Sleep onset latency rose by 9 min and wake after sleep onset by 12 min.
  - Deep sleep (N3/N4) fell by 11.4 min.

### NU-44 Grgic et al. 2021: ISSN position stand on sodium bicarbonate
- J Int Soc Sports Nutr 18 (article number not read). https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-021-00458-w · position stand · ABS
- Finding:
  - Single dose: 0.2 g/kg minimum, 0.3 g/kg optimal, taken 60 to 180 min before exercise. 0.4 to 0.5 g/kg adds nothing and causes more side effects.
  - Benefit is best established for efforts of 30 s to 12 min.
  - Alternative: a multi-day protocol of 0.4 to 0.5 g/kg/day, split across meals, for 3 to 7 days.
  - Gut side effects are common. To reduce them: use a smaller dose, take it about 180 min before, take it with a carbohydrate meal, or use enteric-coated capsules.
  - Not relevant for ultras or long triathlon.
  - At most, it could be trialled for a short hard race (a 5 km or sprint time trial), and only after testing their gut in training.
- Certainty high · Fit low · New

### NU-45 Craven et al. 2021: carbohydrate with or without protein for glycogen resynthesis
- Sports Med Open 7:9. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0 · systematic review and meta-analysis (29 trials, n = 246) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Carbohydrate at about 1.0 g/kg/h vs water: resynthesis +23.5 mmol/kg dry muscle/h, 95% CI 19.0 to 27.9.
  - Hourly feeding beat less frequent feeding (R² 0.44).
  - Carbohydrate plus protein (0.86 plus 0.27 g/kg/h) vs carbohydrate alone (0.95 g/kg/h): +0.4 mmol/kg dm/h, 95% CI -2.7 to 3.4, p = 0.81.
  - Fast refuelling only matters when the next hard session is less than about 8 h away, which is rare for them. In that case take about 1 g/kg/h of carbohydrate in hourly doses.
  - Protein after sessions helps muscle repair, not glycogen.
  - A morning session followed by a noon first meal is fine when the next key session is a day or more away.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### NU-46 Šmid et al. 2024: oral iron supplementation in athletes
- Sports Med 54(5):1231-1247. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-01992-8 · systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of RCTs (13 studies, n = 449) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Ferritin rose: SMD 1.27, 95% CI 0.44 to 2.10.
  - Haemoglobin did not change significantly: SMD 1.31, 95% CI -0.29 to 2.93. Transferrin receptor and transferrin saturation did not change either.

  - Take iron only if a test shows low ferritin. Then do a 6 to 8 week course and retest.
  - No routine iron for a man (see PR-07 on iron excess in runners).
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### NU-48 Khatri et al. 2021: collagen peptides for joints, collagen synthesis and recovery
- Amino Acids 53(10):1493-1506. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00726-021-03072-x · systematic review (15 RCTs: 12 in recreational athletes, 2 in elderly people, 1 in untrained women) · ABS

### NU-49 Lane et al. 2024: ultra-processed food and health outcomes, umbrella review
- BMJ 384:e077310. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310 · umbrella review of 45 pooled analyses (n = 9,888,373) · ABS
- Finding:
  - Higher ultra-processed food exposure was linked to 32 of 45 outcomes.
  - Convincing (class I) evidence:
    - cardiovascular mortality RR 1.50, 95% CI 1.37 to 1.63
    - type 2 diabetes, dose-response, RR 1.12, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.13
    - anxiety OR 1.48 and common mental disorders OR 1.53

    - all-cause mortality RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.27
    - adverse sleep outcomes OR 1.41
  - GRADE certainty was mostly low or very low, because the data are observational.

  - Within the 8 h window, base meals on minimally processed foods.
  - Gels and drink mix during sessions are a separate, functional category.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New

### PR-03 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety dyslipidemia guideline (replaces the 2018 cholesterol guideline)
