**Labels**
- Certainty: how strong the evidence itself is.
- Fit: confidence in applying it to the athlete.
- Access: FT = full text read, ABS = abstract only, SEC = secondary summary only.
- Status:
  - Applied: a current rule rests on it.
  - Informs: context for judgment.
  - Proposed: a rule change waiting for the athlete; see `QUEUE.md`.
  - Watch: mixed or emerging evidence.
  - Superseded: kept for history.
  - New: added by a scan, not yet reported.

## TR: Endurance training and performance

### TR-01 Rosenblat et al. 2025: training intensity distribution, individual-participant network meta-analysis
- Sports Med 55:655–673. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 13 studies, n=348. Polarized and pyramidal did not differ for VO2max (SMD −0.06) or time trial (−0.05). Recreational athletes trended toward pyramidal (SMD 0.32, ns) and competitive athletes toward polarized (−0.31, ns).
- Limits: small arms, 85% male, intensity classified by HR time in zone.
- For the athlete: keep most time easy and allow some tempo. Adherence and easy-day discipline matter more than the model.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-02 Silva Oliveira et al. 2024: polarized vs other distributions, meta-analysis
- Sports Med 54(8):2071–2095. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 17 studies, n=437. Polarized gave slightly more VO2peak gain (SMD 0.24), only in programs under 12 weeks or in highly trained athletes. No time-trial difference (−0.01).
- For the athlete: a short polarized block can lift VO2peak; expect no race-time edge.
- Certainty moderate (TT), low (subgroups) · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-03 Milanović et al. 2015: HIT vs continuous training for VO2max
- Sports Med. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0365-0 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 28 trials, n=723 adults aged 18 to 45. VO2max rose +5.5 mL/kg/min with HIT and +4.9 with continuous training; HIT beat continuous by 1.2. The edge was larger with longer reps and longer programs. Also Wen 2019, J Sci Med Sport 22(8):941, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2019.01.013: only long-interval, high-volume HIIT beat continuous training (SMD 0.65 to 1.07).
- For the athlete: one weekly interval session built on longer reps gives most of the VO2max stimulus within 5 to 7 h/week. VO2max is also a longevity marker (LO-03).
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied (when quality is placed)

### TR-04 Rosenblat et al. 2020: HIIT vs sprint intervals for time trial
- Sports Med 50(6):1145–1161. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01264-1 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 6 studies. Long HIIT (reps of 4 min or more) improved time trial about 2% more than sprint intervals. VO2max did not differ.
- For the athlete: for long-course goals, prefer reps of 3 to 5 min or longer over all-out sprints.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-05 Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024: strength training and running economy
- Sports Med 54(4):895–932. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: n=652 trained runners. Running economy improved with high-load lifting (ES −0.27), plyometrics (−0.31, mainly at 12 km/h or slower) and combined methods (−0.43). Light-load and isometric work had no effect.
- For the athlete: heavy lower-body lifting plus low-volume plyometrics, for at least 6 weeks. Progress calf-loading plyometrics slowly given the calf history.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied (strength rule)

### TR-06 Llanos-Lagos et al. 2025: heavy strength training in cyclists
- Eur J Appl Physiol 126(1):193–222. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-025-05883-2 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: 17 studies, n=262. Heavy lifting at 80% 1RM or more, about 2 sessions/week: cycling performance g=0.46, efficiency g=0.35, VO2max unchanged. Gains needed about 8 weeks or more of 3 to 4 sets across about 4 leg exercises. Supported by Beattie 2014, Sports Med 44(6):845, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0157-y.
- Limits: GRADE low; strength mostly replaced endurance time; strength-naive subjects.
- For the athlete: 2 short heavy sessions in base and build phases, taken from easy volume rather than added on top.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-07 Bosquet et al. 2007: tapering meta-analysis
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 39(8):1358–1365. https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 27 studies. Best effect from a 2-week taper (ES 0.59) with volume cut 41 to 60%, an exponential reduction, and intensity and frequency kept.
- For the athlete: 70.3 taper of about 10 to 14 days, cutting volume 40 to 60%, keeping frequency and short race-pace efforts. Adjust by actual fatigue.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-08 Wang et al. 2023: tapering in endurance athletes
- PLoS One 18(5):e0282838. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282838 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 14 studies. Time trial improved (SMD −0.45). A pre-taper overload plus taper beat a taper alone.
- For the athlete: a modest overload 3 to 5 weeks out, sized to work and study load, then taper.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-09 Düking et al. 2021: HRV-guided training with wearables
- J Sci Med Sport 24(11):1180–1192. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2021.04.012 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 8 studies, n=198. Submaximal markers improved more with HRV guidance (g=0.30). Performance (g=0.08) and VO2peak (g=0.17) were not different.
- For the athlete: HRV is an optional guardrail for swapping hard and easy days on a suppressed multi-day trend, never a gate or a performance tool.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-10 Manresa-Rocamora et al. 2021: HRV-guided training, methodological review
- Int J Environ Res Public Health 18:10299. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph181910299 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: n=199. VO2max (0.13) and performance (0.20) were not different; vagal HRV improved more (0.50), with fewer non-responders. Granero-Gallegos 2020 (https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17217999) found larger effects but with I² of 94%; on the watch list.
- For the athlete: same as TR-09; useful for managing whole-life stress.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### TR-11 Bini et al. 2022: running after cycling
- J Sci Med Sport 25(10):861–866. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2022.07.006 · SR · ABS
- Finding: 39 mostly lab studies. Running after cycling is usually impaired. Changing cadence or intensity gave conflicting results.
- For the athlete: short off-bike runs (10 to 20 min) every 1 to 2 weeks, more often in the last 8 to 10 weeks, mainly to rehearse pacing and fueling. Watch calf load.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs (applies from about March 2027)

### TR-12 Knechtle et al. 2015: what predicts Ironman performance
- Open Access J Sports Med 6:277–290. https://doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S85310 · narrative review · FT
- Finding: training volume predicted race time better than training speed. Bike and run splits drive overall time; the swim does not. Previous race experience predicts 70.3 time.
- For the athlete: prioritize consistent bike and run volume; swim for technique and safety. A tune-up race helps.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs (supports "bike is the largest opportunity")

### TR-13 Wu et al. 2014: pacing in triathlon
- Open Access J Sports Med 5:223–234. https://doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S44392 · narrative review · FT
- Finding: the swim is about 10% of 70.3 time. Even bike pacing works best. Glycogen depletion limits long-course performance.
- For the athlete: an even, capped bike effort with no surges, a conservative run start, and rehearsed race fueling.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### TR-14 Racinais et al. 2023: IOC consensus on sport in the heat
- Br J Sports Med 57:8–25. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-105942 · consensus · FT
- Finding: acclimate with 60 to 90 min/day for at least 2 weeks (4 or more sessions/week to induce, 2/week to maintain). Avoid starting more than 2% dehydrated. For events over 1 h: sodium 0.5 to 0.7 g/L and carbohydrate 30 to 60 g/h. Daanen 2018 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0808-x): adaptations decay about 2.5% per day, and re-induction is fast.
- For the athlete: Eagleman in mid-June is often hot and humid. Plan 10 to 14 days of heat sessions in the final 3 to 4 weeks (overdressed trainer rides or a post-session sauna) and rehearse hydration.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Informs (May 2027)

### TR-15 Iannetta et al. 2020: fixed-percentage intensity prescription vs thresholds
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 52(2):466–473. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002147 · cross-sectional (n=100: 46 women, 54 men) · ABS
- Finding: the lactate threshold fell anywhere from 60 to 90% of HRmax, and maximal lactate steady state from 75 to 97% of HRmax. Fixed %HRmax or %VO2max anchors "conform poorly" to individual intensity domains.
- For the athlete: set zones from their own threshold estimate and confirm with a test, not from a %max formula. Use the talk test (full sentences) as the daily check on the easy ceiling.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied (HR zones, Oct 12)

### TR-16 Spiering et al. 2021: minimal dose to maintain endurance and strength
- J Strength Cond Res 35(5):1449–1458. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/ · narrative review (draws on Hickson and Mujika detraining studies) · ABS
- Finding: endurance performance maintained for up to 15 weeks with training frequency as low as 2 sessions/week, or volume cut by 33 to 66% (about 13 to 26 min per session), provided exercise intensity is maintained. Mostly general populations.
- For the athlete: after Eagleman, a 2-ride weekly bike floor that keeps one short hard set preserves bike fitness through a trail ultra build. Population result; confirm with an indoor power check in late summer.
- Certainty moderate (narrative review of small studies) · Fit moderate · Applied (post-Eagleman bike floor, Oct 17, 2026)

### TR-17 Currier et al. 2026: ACSM position stand on resistance training prescription (overview of reviews)
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4):851–872. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897 · position stand (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants) · SEC (author-shared PDF summary and ACSM infographic; full tables not read)
- Finding: train all major muscle groups at least 2 days/week. Strength: 2 to 3 sets per exercise, heavier loads (about 80% 1RM or more) give more strength (dose-response). Hypertrophy: about 10 or more sets per muscle group per week (dose-response); light (30% 1RM) and heavy loads build similar muscle when effort is matched. Training to failure is not necessary. Going from nothing to any lifting is the biggest gain. Replaces the 2009 ACSM progression model.
- Limits: most underlying data come from untrained or novice lifters; concurrent (strength plus endurance) effects could not be judged for lack of data.
- For the athlete: Long Life Lifts (2x/week, 2 sets per exercise, heavy, not to failure) meets the strength floor. It sits below the ~10 sets/muscle/week hypertrophy dose, which matters for the Feb 1 muscle-rebuild goal, not for the deficit phase. Partly answers open question 7.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · New (scan 2026-11-01)

## IR: Injury, load, recovery and sleep

### IR-01 Soligard et al. 2016: IOC consensus on load and injury
- Br J Sports Med 50(17):1030–1041. https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/17/1030 · consensus · FT
- Finding: large, rapid load changes drive injury risk, and risk stays raised for up to about 4 weeks after a spike. Life stress adds risk. Combine external load, internal load (sRPE) and wellness. Its ACWR thresholds have since been discredited (IR-02).
- For the athlete: describe exposure, change load in small steps, and treat the 3 to 4 weeks after the 50K as a higher-risk window with no new intensity block.
- Certainty moderate (principles), low (numbers) · Fit moderate · Applied

### IR-02 Impellizzeri et al. 2020 and 2021: ACWR critique
- Int J Sports Physiol Perform 15:907–913, https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2019-0864; Sports Med 51(3):581–592, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01378-6 · methodological · ABS
- Finding: ACWR "effects" are a rescaling artifact. Predictive ability was near chance (c=0.57).
- For the athlete: Garmin load ratio and load focus describe trends only.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied

### IR-03 Frandsen et al. 2025: single-session spikes and running injury (Garmin cohort)
- Br J Sports Med 59(17):1203–1210. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-109380 · cohort · ABS
- Finding: 5,205 runners, 588,071 Garmin sessions, 18 months. A single run longer than the longest run in the prior 30 days raised overuse injury risk:
  - 10 to 30% longer: HRR 1.64
  - 30 to 100% longer: HRR 1.52
  - more than 100% longer: HRR 2.28

  Week-to-week distance change and ACWR were not associated. Earlier work, Nielsen 2014 (JOSPT 44(10):739, https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164; SEC), linked weekly increases above 30% to some injuries.
- Limits: self-reported injury; observational.
- For the athlete: the Oct 24 50K (about 31 mi vs a 30-day longest of 12.56 mi) is a more-than-100% spike, a named and deliberate exception. That supports patient pacing, the drop-to-25K option, and a slow rebuild afterward. From 2027, keep single long runs within about 10% of the 30-day longest.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high (same device, adult recreational runners) · Applied (P2, Oct 12)

### IR-04 Videbæk et al. 2015: running injury incidence by runner type
- Sports Med 45(7):1017–1026. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0333-8 · SR/MA · FT
- Finding: novice runners 17.8 injuries per 1000 h, recreational runners 7.7, ultra runners about 7.2.
- For the athlete: about one injury per 130 running hours is the base rate. Treat new terrain (technical descents) as partly novice exposure.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Informs

### IR-05 Vuurberg et al. 2018 (ankle sprain guideline) and Smith et al. 2021 (PAASS return-to-sport consensus)
- Br J Sports Med 52(15):956, https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/15/956; Br J Sports Med 55(22):1270, https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-104087 · guideline + Delphi consensus · FT
- Finding: exercise therapy cuts recurrence (RR 0.62) and bracing cuts it further (RR 0.30). PAASS return domains: pain (during sport and over 24 h), impairments (range of motion, strength), athlete perception, sensorimotor control (balance) and functional performance (hops, a full session).
- For the athlete: their Oct 10 issue is medial ("below the inside ankle"), not a diagnosed lateral sprain, so the sprain-specific numbers do not transfer. The PAASS domains still frame a sound return test: pain-free walking, heel raises and hops, then a session with no next-day worsening. That is the current plan.
- Certainty high (sprain care), moderate (PAASS) · Fit low to moderate · Informs

### IR-06 Green and Pizzari 2017: calf strain risk factors
- Br J Sports Med 51(16):1189–1194. https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/16/1189 · SR · ABS
- Finding: older age and a previous calf strain are the strongest risk factors. One study found a calf injury in the prior 8 weeks gave RR 8.9.
- For the athlete: the athlete stays a higher calf-risk athlete. Heavy straight-knee and bent-knee calf raises 2x/week, and conservatism with hills and speed after any calf symptom.
- Certainty low to moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### IR-07 Lauersen et al. 2014 and 2018: strength training prevents sports injuries
- Br J Sports Med 48(11):871, https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871; Br J Sports Med 52(24):1557, https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099078 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 2014: 25 RCTs, n=26,610. Strength training RR 0.32, proprioception RR 0.55, stretching RR 0.96 (no effect); overuse injuries RR 0.53. 2018: each 10% increase in strength volume cut risk by more than 4 percentage points.
- For the athlete: 2 short strength sessions a week (calf, hip, single-leg) even in peak weeks. Stretching is optional for comfort, not prevention.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate (mostly team sport and youth) · Applied (P1, Oct 12)

### IR-08 Meeusen et al. 2013: overtraining syndrome, ECSS/ACSM consensus
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 45(1):186–205. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e318279a10a · consensus · FT
- Finding: functional overreaching lasts days to about 2 weeks; non-functional overreaching weeks to months; overtraining syndrome months or more. No single marker identifies it. Prevention: training logs, rest days, sleep, carbohydrate, avoiding monotony, mood tracking.
- For the athlete: at least one low-load day a week. A performance stall over 2 weeks with mood or sleep change despite an easy week means cutting load and considering a clinician visit.
- Certainty moderate (definitions) · Fit moderate · Applied

### IR-09 Mountjoy et al. 2023: IOC consensus on REDs
- Br J Sports Med 57(17):1073–1097. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994 · consensus · FT
- Finding: problematic low energy availability harms health and performance. The male threshold appears lower (about 9 to 25 kcal/kg FFM/day). Only about 20% of studies included males. Emerging male indicators: low libido and fewer morning erections; clinically low testosterone is severe. Bone health is affected.
- For the athlete: modest deficit only; never in key blocks, taper or race recovery; fuel long sessions fully. Persistent fatigue, illness, stalled performance, bone pain or low libido mean pausing the deficit and seeing a clinician. Never diagnosed from a watch.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### IR-10 Walsh et al. 2021: sleep and the athlete, expert consensus
- Br J Sports Med 55(7):356–368. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102025 · consensus · ABS. Also Bonnar 2018, Sports Med 48(3):683, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0832-x: sleep extension had the most benefit for performance.
- Finding: habitual sleep under 7 h counts as short. A fixed 7 to 9 h rule is "unlikely ideal", so needs should be individualized. Short sleep raises infection risk in the general population.
- For the athlete: their calibration (6.5 to 6.75 h, about 78 to 83 score) is their normal, but Garmin likely overstates it (IR-12). Protect sleep opportunity (about 8 h in bed) in hard weeks, race week and study crunch weeks. After a very short night, swap intensity for easy volume.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied; Watch (calibration)

### IR-11 Mesas et al. 2023: daytime napping and performance
- Br J Sports Med 57(7):417. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-106355 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 22 RCTs, men aged 18 to 35. A nap improved physical performance (SMD 0.99) and reduced fatigue. Best naps were 30 to 60 min, with more than 1 h before training.
- For the athlete: on WFH days after a short night, a 30 to 45 min early-afternoon nap is a legitimate recovery tool.
- Certainty low to moderate · Fit moderate · Informs

### IR-12 Wearable validity: Miller 2022; Schyvens 2024 and 2025; Dial 2025 (watch)
- Sensors 22(16):6317, https://doi.org/10.3390/s22166317; Sleep Adv 6(2):zpaf021, https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaf021; JMIR 2024 review, https://doi.org/10.2196/52192 · validation studies · FT/ABS
- Finding:
  - Garmin overestimated total sleep by about 38 to 47 min and caught only about 28% of wake epochs.
  - Four-stage agreement was poor (kappa about 0.2), with deep-sleep sensitivity of 28%.
  - Daytime 3-min HRV agreed poorly with ECG (ICC 0.24).
  - Dial 2025 (Physiol Rep, https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70527, ABS): overnight HRV on the fenix 6 was better (CCC 0.87, MAPE 10.5%).
- For the athlete: read sleep duration as a trend and assume actual sleep is about 30 to 45 min less than shown. Do not flag sleep stages (deep, REM) as findings. Overnight HRV is usable as a 7-day trend.
- Certainty moderate (sleep), low to moderate (HRV) · Fit high (their device class) · Applied (P3, Oct 12)

### IR-13 Scheer and Krabak 2021: ultra-running injuries (scoping review)
- Front Physiol 12:664071. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.664071 · scoping review · FT. Also Knechtle and Nikolaidis 2018, https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2018.00634.
- Finding: 32.8% of runners in a 65 km trail race had a musculoskeletal injury, with ankle sprains and plantar fasciitis each 28.6% of injuries. Muscle-damage markers soar during an ultra and usually normalize afterward.
- For the athlete: protect ankles on descents and do not race through a gait change. After the 50K, 1 to 2 weeks of mostly swim and bike before running intensity returns. That timing is coaching judgment; the review gives no number.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied (50K plan)

### IR-14 Gillinov et al. 2017: accuracy of wearable heart rate monitors during aerobic exercise
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 49(8):1697–1703. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28709155/ · validation study · SEC (title verified; PubMed page blocked)
- Finding: an ECG-grade chest strap was the most accurate. Wrist optical accuracy varied by device and activity and was worse with arm movement and at higher intensity.
- For the athlete: wrist HR on hard runs may read high or lock to cadence. The Sep 3 run (10:00 to 10:40/mi at 176 to 186 bpm) looks suspicious (Inferred). A chest strap makes threshold tests and zone work trustworthy.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Informs (HR zones)

### IR-15 Saw, Main and Gastin 2016: subjective vs objective measures of training response
- Br J Sports Med 50(5):281–291. https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/5/281 (PMC4789708) · SR · SEC (title and journal verified; findings as widely reported)
- Finding: across 56 studies, athlete self-reported well-being (mood, fatigue, soreness, stress, sleep quality) responded to acute and chronic training load with better sensitivity and consistency than common objective markers (blood markers, HR measures). Well-being worsened with load increases and improved with load reductions.
- For the athlete: their daily 1 to 10 feel scores carry at least as much weight as Garmin readiness metrics.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied (spec 5a, Oct 12)

### IR-16 Haddad et al. 2017: the session-RPE method (review), building on Foster et al. 2001
- Front Neurosci 11:612. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00612 · narrative review · FT. Foster et al. 2001, J Strength Cond Res 15(1):109–115, is the original method.
- Finding: 36 validity and reliability studies. Session RPE (CR-10, whole session, rated retrospectively) correlates well with HR-based load (r of about 0.5 to 0.97 across sports; weaker for some resistance and intermittent work). Load = RPE x minutes. Athletes should be familiarized with the scale.
- For the athlete: one number per session (RPE about 30 min after) gives a valid internal load with no chest strap. It becomes the main weekly load number.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied (spec 5a)

### IR-17 Vieira et al. 2022: the talk test (systematic review)
- Rev Cardiovasc Med 23(7):225. https://doi.org/10.31083/j.rcm2307225 · SR · ABS
- Finding: the last stage at which talking stays comfortable matched the first ventilatory threshold (VT1) for workload, VO2 and HR. Protocols are not standardized, and reliability data are limited.
- Limits: the population was cardiopulmonary patients; similar findings exist in healthy adults but were not re-verified here.
- For the athlete: "full sentences" is a sound, practical ceiling for easy days, consistent with training by feel.
- Certainty low to moderate · Fit moderate · Applied (spec 5a)

### IR-18 Lipman et al. 2017: ibuprofen vs placebo and acute kidney injury in an ultramarathon (RCT)
- Emerg Med J 34(10):637–642. https://emj.bmj.com/content/34/10/637; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28679502/; NCT02272725 · RCT · ABS
- Finding: 89 runners in a 50-mile (80 km) stage of multi-day desert ultras, ibuprofen 400 mg every 4 h vs placebo. Acute kidney injury (creatinine-based) 52% with ibuprofen vs 34% with placebo; number needed to harm about 5.5; AKI was also more severe. Underpowered, so the difference was not statistically significant (OR 2.1, 95% CI 0.9 to 5.1).
- Limits: baseline creatinine estimated, not measured; multi-stage desert races; small, with high attrition.
- For the athlete: kidney stress is common in ultras even without drugs (about a third on placebo), and ibuprofen during a race likely adds to it. Supports "no NSAIDs during or right after long races"; use other pain plans. Answers the NSAID part of the ultra acute-risk question; rhabdomyolysis incidence remains open.
- Certainty low to moderate · Fit moderate · New (scan 2026-11-01)

## NU: Nutrition, fueling, hydration and body composition

### NU-01 Thomas et al. 2016: ACSM/AND/DC joint position on nutrition and athletic performance
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 48(3):543–568. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852 · consensus · FT
- Finding:
  - Carbohydrate: 3 to 5 g/kg on light days, 5 to 7 on about 1 h/day, 6 to 10 on 1 to 3 h/day.
  - Protein: 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg, about 0.3 g/kg every 3 to 5 h.
  - During exercise: 30 to 60 g/h carbohydrate for 1 to 2.5 h, up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h.
  - Fluid: keep losses under 2% of body mass.
  - Weight loss: about 250 to 500 kcal/day deficit, in base season.
  - Energy availability of about 45 kcal/kg FFM is balanced; below about 30 impairs function.
- For the athlete: carbohydrate scales with the day; cut it only on easy days. The deficit stays at or under about 500 kcal/day and lives in base season.
- Certainty high · Fit high · Applied

### NU-02 Jäger et al. 2017: ISSN position stand on protein and exercise
- J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:20. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8 · position stand · FT
- Finding: 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day; 20 to 40 g per dose every 3 to 4 h; 30 to 40 g casein before sleep raises overnight protein synthesis. Higher intake plus resistance training helps preserve lean mass in a deficit.
- For the athlete: 3 to 4 feeds of 30 to 40 g inside the 8 h window, with a slow protein (dairy) near window close.
- Certainty moderate (ISSN has industry ties) · Fit high · Applied

### NU-03 Morton et al. 2018: protein and resistance-training gains
- Br J Sports Med 52(6):376–384. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 49 RCTs, n=1,863. No added lean mass above about 1.6 g/kg/day (95% CI upper bound about 2.2).
- For the athlete: 1.6 g/kg (about 110 g) is the floor; 120 to 150 g covers the upper bound.
- Certainty high · Fit moderate · Applied

### NU-04 Helms et al. 2014: protein during caloric restriction in lean athletes
- Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 24(2):127–138. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2013-0054 · SR · ABS
- Finding: estimated need is 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg FFM in a deficit, rising with leanness and deficit size.
- For the athlete: in a deficit, sit near 150 g/day.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### NU-05 Longland et al. 2016: high vs lower protein in a large deficit
- Am J Clin Nutr 103(3):738–746. https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.119339 · RCT · ABS
- Finding: 40 young men, 4 weeks at about a 40% deficit. 2.4 g/kg gave +1.2 kg lean mass vs +0.1 kg with 1.2 g/kg, and more fat loss.
- For the athlete: high protein matters most in big deficits; their small deficit is covered by about 2.0 to 2.2 g/kg.
- Certainty moderate · Fit low to moderate · Informs

### NU-06 Jeukendrup 2014 and Burke et al. 2011: carbohydrate during exercise
- Sports Med 44(Suppl 1):S25–33, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0148-z; J Sports Sci 29(S1):S17, https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.585473 · review / consensus-level guidance · ABS
- Finding: about 30 to 60 g/h for 1 to 2.5 h; up to 90 g/h for events over 2.5 h using glucose plus fructose. Not scaled to body weight.
- For the athlete: 70.3 race and long bikes 60 to 90 g/h (gels plus drink); easy long runs and hikes 30 to 60 g/h of real food.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied

### NU-07 Costa et al. 2017 and Jeukendrup 2017: training the gut
- Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 42(5):547–557, https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2016-0453 (RCT, ABS); Sports Med 47(Suppl 1):101, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0690-6 (review)
- Finding: 2 weeks of repeated gut challenges at 90 g/h cut GI symptoms by about 60% and improved a 1 h distance test by about 4 to 5%.
- For the athlete: from about 8 to 10 weeks before Eagleman, weekly long sessions at race fueling rates with the exact race products, built up gradually.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied (spring 2027)

### NU-08 Tiller et al. 2019: ISSN position stand on ultra-marathon nutrition
- J Int Soc Sports Nutr 16:50. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-019-0312-9 · position stand · FT
- Finding: in the race, 150 to 400 kcal/h with 30 to 50 g/h carbohydrate and some protein; fluid 450 to 750 mL/h with sodium above 575 mg/L; caffeine in small repeated doses late.
- For the athlete (50K): plan by calories (about 200 to 400 kcal/h) using rehearsed products; savory real food is fine; more sodium in heat.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied (Oct 24)

### NU-09 Garthe et al. 2011: weight-loss rate in athletes
- Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 21(2):97–104. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.21.2.97 · RCT · ABS
- Finding: n=24 elite athletes. Losing 0.7% of body weight per week preserved and grew lean mass (+2.1%) and performance better than about 1.0% per week.
- For the athlete: the cap is about 0.7% of body weight per week (about 1 lb). Their plan of 0.3 to 0.6 lb per week sits safely under it.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied

### NU-10 Lowe et al. 2020: the TREAT randomized trial of 16:8 eating
- JAMA Intern Med 180(11):1491–1499. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153 · RCT · ABS
- Finding: n=116 adults with overweight, 12 weeks. 16:8 with no calorie target gave −0.94 kg vs −0.68 kg (difference not significant). Appendicular lean mass fell more with 16:8.
- For the athlete: 16:8 alone does not produce fat loss; the deficit does. Protect lean mass with protein and 2x/week strength.
- Certainty moderate · Fit low to moderate · Applied

### NU-11 Liu et al. 2022: calorie restriction with or without time-restricted eating
- N Engl J Med 386:1495–1504. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35443107/ · RCT · SEC
- Finding: n=139 adults with obesity, 12 months. TRE plus calorie restriction lost −8.0 kg vs −6.3 kg for calorie restriction alone (not significant).
- For the athlete: keep 16:8 because it suits them, not for an added fat-loss effect.
- Certainty high (for the question) · Fit low · Informs

### NU-12 Moro et al. 2016 and 2020: time-restricted eating in trained men and elite cyclists
- J Transl Med 14:290, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-016-1044-0; J Int Soc Sports Nutr 17:65, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-020-00396-z · RCTs · ABS. Also Conde-Pipó 2024 SR, https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16010168.
- Finding: with energy needs met inside the window, fat fell, lean mass and performance held, and free testosterone, IGF-1 and T3 fell.
- For the athlete: 16:8 is compatible if energy and carbohydrate are met. Shift the window so key sessions are fed and followed by a recovery meal, and relax it on long days. Hormone drops make it a watch item alongside REDs signs.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied; Watch

### NU-13 Aird et al. 2018: fasted vs fed exercise
- Scand J Med Sci Sports 28(5):1476–1493. https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13054 · SR/MA · ABS
- Finding: 46 studies. Eating before exercise improved prolonged aerobic performance but not shorter sessions. Fasted training raised fat-use signaling, with no proven long-term performance benefit.
- For the athlete: fasted sessions stay easy and under about 60 to 90 min. Eat before long, threshold, race-pace and brick sessions.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied

### NU-14 Sawka et al. 2007: ACSM position stand on fluid replacement
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 39(2):377–390. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/ · position stand · ABS
- Finding: avoid losing more than 2% of body mass; individualize with sweat-rate checks; electrolytes and carbohydrate help in long sessions.
- For the athlete: one or two sweat-rate checks in race-like heat in spring 2027; add sodium for sessions over 2 h.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Informs

### NU-15 Hew-Butler et al. 2015: exercise-associated hyponatremia consensus
- Br J Sports Med 49(22):1432–1446. https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/49/22/1432 (also https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000221) · consensus · ABS
- Finding: overdrinking low-sodium fluid is the main cause. Drinking to thirst is the recommended prevention. Sodium does not prevent hyponatremia from overdrinking.
- For the athlete: drink to thirst within a planned ceiling, and never finish heavier than at the start.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied

### NU-16 Guest et al. 2021: ISSN position stand on caffeine
- J Int Soc Sports Nutr 18:1. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4 · position stand · ABS
- Finding: 3 to 6 mg/kg improves endurance; 2 mg/kg may suffice; 9 mg/kg adds side effects. Usually taken 60 min before; gum and gels act faster.
- For the athlete: test 2 to 3 mg/kg (about 140 to 210 mg) in training before any race use. Avoid late-day doses that cost sleep.
- Certainty high · Fit moderate · Informs

### NU-17 Maughan et al. 2018 (IOC consensus on supplements) and Senefeld et al. 2020 (nitrate)
- Br J Sports Med 52(7):439–455, https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/7/439; Med Sci Sports Exerc 52(10):2250, https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002363 · consensus + SR/MA
- Finding: only caffeine, creatine, nitrate, bicarbonate and possibly beta-alanine have good evidence. About 15% of supplements contained undeclared prohormones. Nitrate gave a small effect (d=0.17) in recreational athletes.
- For the athlete: food first. Use only third-party-tested products. Nitrate is an optional low-yield trial. No supplement is ever started without a reason to.
- Certainty high · Fit moderate · Informs

### NU-18 Poon et al. 2025 (diet breaks meta-analysis) and the ICECAP 1-week diet-break analysis (PLoS One 2021)
- Nutr Rev 83(1):59–71, https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuad168 · SR/MA · ABS. PLoS One 16(2):e0247292, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247292 · pre-specified secondary analysis of an RCT · ABS
- Finding: 12 RCTs, n=881 (mostly non-athletes). Intermittent restriction with break periods at maintenance gave the same weight and fat loss as continuous restriction, with a smaller drop in resting metabolic rate. In 26 resistance-trained athletes, a 1-week maintenance break during a 12-week diet raised leg muscle endurance, alertness and fullness, cut hunger and irritability, and did not change fat mass (body mass +0.6 kg, mostly glycogen and water).
- Limits: few trained or endurance populations; short trials; abstracts only.
- For the athlete: a maintenance week does not cost fat loss and may help appetite and leg training. The planned Dec 21 to 27 maintenance week already works as one. If hunger or leg quality slips in January, a maintenance week is an evidence-backed lever. No change to P5.
- Certainty low to moderate · Fit moderate · New (scan 2026-11-01)

### LO-05 Momma et al. 2022: muscle-strengthening activity and mortality

### LO-07 Lee et al. 2022 and Arem et al. 2015: activity dose and mortality

### LO-10 Newman et al. 2021 and Andersen et al. 2013: atrial fibrillation in athletes

### LO-16 Cannon et al. 2026: advanced adenomas in marathon and ultramarathon runners

- Scan 2026-11-01 (SEC: ACC journal scan, Quest and 2 Minute Medicine summaries): Blumenthal RS et al., J Am Coll Cardiol 2026, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016; also Circulation 2026;153(17):1154–1276. Class and level of evidence per recommendation still not read.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied (ask list); content verified from secondary summaries, COR/LOE still unverified

### PR-04 Lp(a): EAS consensus 2022 and NLA update 2024
- Eur Heart J, https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehac361; J Clin Lipidol, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacl.2024.03.001 · consensus
- Finding: test once in adulthood. 125 nmol/L or more is high risk. Lifestyle barely changes Lp(a); there is no approved lowering drug with proven outcome benefit yet.
- For the athlete: a one-time test. A high result means tighter control of everything else, and is the clinician's call.
- Scan 2026-11-01: Lp(a)HORIZON (pelacarsen, >8,000 patients with established CVD and high Lp(a)) missed its primary endpoint of major CV events in topline results announced Sept 4, 2026; full data not yet presented (SEC: https://www.tctmd.com/news/lpahorizon-top-line-results-dash-hopes-pelacarsen; https://www.healio.com/news/cardiology/20260908/not-the-results-we-hoped-for-lpalowering-drug-pelacarsen-fails-to-reduce-cv-events). Olpasiran, lepodisiran and zerlasiran outcome trials continue. No change for the athlete: the one-time test still tells them how tightly to manage LDL, blood pressure and the rest.
- Certainty moderate · Fit high · Applied; Watch (outcome trials)

### PR-05 Riebe et al. 2015: ACSM pre-participation screening
- Med Sci Sports Exerc 47(11):2473–2479. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000664 · consensus
- Finding: active, symptom-free people with no known disease need no clearance. New symptoms mean stopping and getting medical clearance: chest, jaw or arm discomfort; breathlessness at rest or on mild effort; dizziness or fainting; palpitations; unusual fatigue.
- For the athlete: no clearance needed to train. These symptoms are on the red-flag list.
- Certainty low (expert) · Fit high · Applied

### PR-06 Pelliccia et al. 2020: ESC guidelines on sports cardiology
- Eur Heart J 42(1):17–96. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaa605 · guideline
- Finding: low-risk recreational athletes over 35 need no routine evaluation. For competitive athletes, consider family history, symptoms, an exam and a 12-lead ECG (IIa, C).
- For the athlete: at the physical, report any family history of sudden cardiac death or early heart disease, and ask whether a resting ECG is reasonable given endurance racing.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Applied (ask list)

### PR-07 Sim et al. 2019 (iron in athletes) and Mettler and Zimmermann 2010 (iron excess in runners)
- Eur J Appl Physiol, https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-019-04157-y; Eur J Clin Nutr 64:490, https://doi.org/10.1038/ejcn.2010.16 · review + cross-sectional
- Finding: iron deficiency affects about 3 to 11% of male athletes. 15% of male marathoners had iron excess. Supplements do not help unless deficient.
- For the athlete: never take iron without testing. Ferritin, Hb and transferrin saturation are reasonable to ask for at the physical or with unexplained fatigue, drawn in the morning and over 24 h after hard training.
- Certainty moderate · Fit moderate · Applied

### PR-09 Noetel et al. 2024: exercise for depression, network meta-analysis
- BMJ 384:e075847. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847 · SR/MA
- Finding: 218 RCTs. Walking or jogging g −0.62, strength −0.49; larger with higher intensity. Certainty low.
- For the athlete: training supports mental health; it is not a treatment. Low mood lasting about 2 weeks or more points to a clinician.
- Certainty low · Fit moderate · Informs

### PR-10 Singh et al. 2023: physical activity for depression, anxiety and distress (umbrella review)

### PR-11 Stults-Kolehmainen et al. 2014: psychological stress and recovery

### PR-13 Lally et al. 2010 and Singh et al. 2024: how long habits take to form
- Eur J Soc Psychol 40(6):998, https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674; Healthcare 12(23):2488, https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488 · cohort + SR/MA
- Finding: median about 59 to 66 days to automaticity (exercise habits about 91), with a wide range. A missed day did not derail a habit.
- For the athlete: frame a new habit (strength, steps) as a 2 to 5 month project on a stable cue; after a miss, just resume.
